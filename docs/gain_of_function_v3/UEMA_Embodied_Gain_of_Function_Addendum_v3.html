1. What We Are Trying to Prove

Plan text (v2, unchanged)

The first UEMA model should learn a simple loop:

I intend to do something
→
I predict what will happen
→
I act
→
I sense what happened
→
I update my model

A real gain of function is not “the arm moved” or “the eye jumped.” It is:

PredictThe model says what should happen before it acts.
IntendThe action is selected because of its predicted consequence.
ExecuteThe predicted consequence actually happens.
RepeatThe system can make it happen again.
Main thesis: early UEMA learning should strongly value embodied controllability — knowing that “when I choose this action, my body and senses change in the way I expect.”

2. Why Self-on-Self Contact Matters

Plan text (v2, unchanged)

MIMo now has self-on-self contact. In the rigid-body simulator, useful signals include contact force and shear-like contact information tied back to body parts. That gives the model more than joint angles alone.

Self-contact is unusually useful because the same event is sensed on both sides of the body. When one finger touches another, both fingers feel it, both finger poses are known, the motor command is known, and vision may also see the event.

Implementation point: weight successful prediction and control of self-contact more strongly than ordinary external contact during early body learning. Do not give wellness merely because self-contact happened. Otherwise the model could learn a useless loop of touching itself repeatedly.

MIMo versus the Unreal Engine avatar

MIMo nowUnreal Engine avatar later
Rigid-body joint state, body pose, contact force, and shear-like contact signals.All of the above plus richer soft-body deformation, local stretch/strain, skin movement, and non-contact proprioceptive deformation signals.

The same UEMA logic can be used in both systems, but the Unreal avatar can provide a richer proprioceptive stream.

3. Wellness Should Care About Control — But Not Treat All Surprise as Bad

Plan text (v2, unchanged)

3.1 Embodied controllability

A failed body prediction should matter when it means loss of agency. Example: “I expected my elbow to bend, but it did not.”

3.2 Do not make “surprise = bad”

If every surprising event lowers wellness, the safest strategy can become “stay still and avoid new things.” Separate:

  • Control failure: I intended an effect, but my body did not do it.
  • Harmful internal state: too much strain, fatigue, instability, or unsafe force.
  • Useful novelty: something unexpected happened, but it helped the model learn.

3.3 Learning progress

The model should care not only about having low prediction error, but also about getting better.

A simple measure is: old prediction error − new prediction error. If the number is positive, the model is learning that part of its world.

3.4 Wellness should have priorities

  1. Body integrity and safety — strong strain, damaging force, or unstable internal state can override everything else.
  2. Controllability — can intended body effects actually be produced?
  3. Learning progress — is prediction improving?
  4. Efficiency — among movements that work, prefer lower effort and lower fatigue.

This is still a wellness system, not a task reward or policy network.

3.1 Controllability — --controllability on, commit 664415b95

For each order, the effect the model expected (the efference body head's 250 ms prediction; the commanded turn when those heads are off) is compared with the effect the body achieved over the same 250 ms. An order counts only when both are larger than the drift of a body told to hold still (the drift that only 5 in 100 hold-still orders exceeded). A mispaired control compares one body's expectation with another body's result.

  • Measured before, from the commanded turn (1 body, 136 orders): the expected and achieved effects agreed no better than one body's expectation agreed with another body's result (the agreement score multiplies direction match by size match; not yet measured as a count). The orders asked for 57° of turn per axis; the handles turned 12°.
  • With the fresh efference head, the expected effect stayed under the hold-still drift on 78 of 78 orders, so the channel reads "unmeasured" until the head learns.
  • Added beyond the design (a deviation, stated): the channel's weight stays 0 until its last 32 readings beat the mispaired control by more than twice their standard error, because in the build's run the reading sat inside the control's own range and would have steered goals by noise.
  • Review finding (major), fix in progress: the control swaps in another body's whole expectation, which also carries that body's own state. A head that predicts each body's drift from its state alone, and ignores the order, then beats the control and opens the gate: in the reviewer's test it opened after 8 orders. The fix scores this body's state with another body's order instead, which b1's heads already compute.

3.2 Surprise — now split into three channels

  • Control failure is the controllability channel above.
  • Harmful internal state is the integrity channel: 1 minus the larger of skin pain and the penalty for pushing a limb to the edge of its reach. It is also a gate: a remembered moment more painful than the most painful of 980 ordinary lived moments is never chosen as a goal. On the pain scale (0% = no modelled aversion anywhere on the skin, 100% = the extreme-load end, never reached) that line is 87%; two 2-body runs peaked at 75% and 78%, and routine pain on a settled body sits near 77%.
  • Useful novelty is learning progress (3.3). It replaces the curiosity multiplier, which before the build decided the winning goal in 5 of 5 captured snapshots; curiosity is now one added channel with a quarter of controllability's weight.
  • Still true: the old prediction-error formula (1 minus the error) remains in the raw wellness sum and as a channel with a quarter of controllability's weight, so every surprise still lowers that one channel.

3.3 Learning progress — --learning-progress on, commit 664415b95

Per sense and per body, the model's error is divided by the error of predicting "nothing changes", which cancels the world simply becoming easier or harder (the caregiver leaving). Progress is how far the recent (200-step) average of that ratio sits below its long-run (2,000-step) average: 0% = not changing, 100% = the recent error has vanished, below 0% = getting worse.

  • During training (2 bodies, 240 moments): 0.6% and 1.2% per 100-moment window; vision 3.1% and 7.6%.
  • On frozen weights, where nothing can be learned, it read −1.6% to −7.1%, from the world changing alone. So a reading of this size does not prove learning. open A fixed-anchor control (a frozen copy scoring the same moments, "old error minus new error") is not built; its cost is one more copy of the forward model and heads in memory and one extra forward pass per sampled moment. The fix lane for b3 carries it.

3.4 Priorities — --wellness-rank normalized, commit 664415b95

Goal choice now ranks remembered moments by value = Σ weight × (channel − its running centre) ÷ max(its running spread, its measured floor), with the integrity gate above. The raw wellness sum is unchanged; it still feeds the reflex controller's input and the self-model's teacher (the entity self, which tracks which named things go with wellness).

  • Review blocker: the ranking picks goals by when a moment happened, not by what it holds. In the build's own run (2 bodies, 240 moments, training), 42 of 44 goals were in the oldest tenth of the 200-moment ring; a choice blind to age puts 4.4 of 44 there on average. Integrity, capability and learning progress drift steadily across a ring's 10 s, so their sum peaks at one end of it. Taking those three out of the re-ranking brought the oldest tenth down to 16 in 100 goals. At the deploy the fresh heads' learning progress would pull the goal to the present moment, telling the planner to stay where it is. Not to be switched on until fixed; the fix is in the model lane.
  • In the same run, integrity carried 97 to 99 of every 100 parts of how much the ranking value varied between moments, while pain was still building from 49% to 78% of the way to the extreme-load end. The record had no field for the chosen goal's age, which is why this went unseen.
  • Also found (major): the list of ranked channels is saved with the episode, so a later resume that changes a wellness switch either stops with an error at the first step or silently ignores the change.
  • Efficiency is logged with weight 0: energy is item 11, outside this build.

4. Energy, Strength, Fatigue, and Body Limits

Plan text (v2, unchanged)

Energy matters because raising an arm against gravity, holding the body up, or repeatedly using an actuator should not be free. But energy should be a secondary shaper early in development.

  • Predict the effort/fatigue consequence of an intended movement.
  • Keep the early penalty modest.
  • As controllability improves, let energy shape the style of movement.
  • Let strength, fatigue, range of motion, and prior use change what movements are possible.

Energy tests

  • Does predicted effort rise when the arm is lifted against gravity?
  • Does holding the arm up continue to cost energy?
  • Does a tired actuator change the predicted movement?
  • Can the model find a lower-effort way to get the same body result?
Use the energy model that already exists in MIMo as the first test bed. Implement the Unreal equivalent later; do not let that block the earlier controllability work.

5. Soft Motor Development: Synergies, Not Hard Joint Unlocking

Plan text (v2, unchanged)

We considered turning joints on in a hand-written schedule. That is not the preferred design. Biology is closer to broad early movement patterns that become more differentiated with learning.

Compact UEMA motor intention
→
Small nonlinear motor-synergy MLP
→
Many joint / actuator commands
→
Body movement
  • Use a small nonlinear MLP immediately rather than spending weeks proving a linear version first.
  • Input: compact motor intention.
  • Use 2–3 small nonlinear layers for the first version.
  • Output: motor targets or motor deltas.
  • Train it as part of the embodied loop.

The MLP is not a policy network. It is a low-level coordination map. Early control can be broad and coupled; finer independent control should emerge as prediction improves.

Status in v3
built behind flags (80eaf88ac); not yet in a running segment review running (review-b5) measured: following counts, physics check

Built (commit 80eaf88ac; work log agents/b5-synergy.md): the network the plan asks for, with two nonlinear hidden layers. It turns an intention of 32 numbers into a change of every body handle's target, relative to where the body is now: a turn for each of the 25 body handles and a move for each of the 14 handles whose position the decoder reads, never the eyes (they have their own servo); all zeros hold the pose.

  • How it learns, in the loop: every order that ran its whole 250 ms becomes a sample: the pose it began in and the change the body really made. The network must reproduce that change through its 32 numbers. Each sample counts in proportion to how well the order was followed and how much the body moved, so orders the body ignored do not shape it. Nothing here is a reward.
  • Who uses it: the planner searches the 32 numbers instead of 234 numbers per body (--motor-synergy on); babble draws the 32 numbers at random (--synergy-babble on), sized so each body handle turns 45.7° on average, the same as today's separate wobble. With --smooth-source executed the smoother blends the new order with the order the body really got; today it blends with a running average of proposals, most of them never sent.
  • Why 32 numbers: on the record saved with the last checkpoint (9,592 orders that ran their whole 250 ms, 8 bodies), 32 directions carry 89 of every 100 parts of the recorded posture changes; 90 would need 35, and the design caps the size at 32. Trained on the first 80% of that record in time, it reproduced 83% of each later change it had not seen (0% = no better than asking for no change, 100% = the whole change); 16 numbers reached 79%, 8 numbers 70%.
  • At the switch: a fresh network asks 0.0069° of turn and 0.0018 cm of move per handle, against 11° and 2.8 cm for one typical 250 ms change, so at first the planner's orders hold the pose; after the smoother they ask 14° of turn per handle, against 40° from today's planner (1 body, processor). Emulated on the recorded orders, it reproduced 38% of a change before learning from it after 1,000 samples, and 71% to 76% after 5,000 to 9,000.
  • Proofs on the real checkpoint (processor, 1 body): with the switches off, every order, loss and weight equals the code before it; switched on, only the new network starts fresh, and 406 of 406 saved optimizer states carry over.
  • Cost: 4 ms per moment on average on the processor; a lesson every fifth moment takes 11 ms.
  • No hard schedule exists: H84 runs no growth ramp and no joint unlocking.

In plain words: what the synergy network is, why, and whether it can give exact movement (operator request, 2026-09-23; updated with the following counts and the build's status)

Where it stands. Segment 32 is training the synergy map with its planner and exploration off and proposal smoothing retained. The review found a regression when the fresh map and executed-order smoothing were enabled together. The planner now starts from the encoded controller proposal; activating it still requires the paired physical comparison in the takeover criteria. Faster learning remains unproven.

How is the body moved today? The model never sets joints directly. Each order gives each of 25 body parts (plus the eyes) a target: where the part should be and how it should be turned. A solver (inverse kinematics, IK: working out the joint angles that reach a target) turns those targets into joint forces, solving each arm as one connected chain. So IK is already used, and these targets are the same idea as Unreal's physics handles. One piece is missing: the spec gives each handle a radius (how far its pull spreads to nearby bones), and the MIMo decoder never reads it.

How well does the body follow its orders? The count: is a body part closer to its ordered orientation 250 ms after the order than it was when the order was given? Out of 100 orders:

  • Live orders: 54. These are the last checkpoint's own orders, from 5 of its 8 bodies over 12.5 s each (6,125 part-orders).
  • A pose the body has held: 87. Physics only, no model: the body is driven into a pose, brought back, then ordered to that pose. These turns are small: median 4.6°, against 23.8° for the live orders.
  • Separate random turns: 69. Physics only: every part gets its own random 20° turn, the kind of order babble makes.
  • Chance: 31. That is what the same real turns score against a target at the same distance in a random direction. It is not 50, because a turn about as large as the distance lands closer to a random target only about 1 time in 4.

Limits of these counts: the live count covers 5 of 8 bodies, because the speech library the world needs was deleted from disk mid-run (and later restored). The probe learns nothing during its 12.5 s; the live run learns every step. The physics cases have no caregiver and no fatigue and use fixed sizes, so they show what the body's mechanics allow; they are not goal lines for the live orders.

Why random moves at all? UEMA gets no demonstrations and no reward, so the model must find out what its own orders do by trying them. That is motor babbling, a standard method in developmental robotics and part of the UEMA spec (section 19.2).

What is wrong with today's random moves? Each part's target gets its own random twist, chosen with no regard to any other part. Under separate random turns the forearms end closer only 49 to 52 times in 100, against 96 to 98 for a pose the body has held. The held-pose orders ask smaller turns, but even the quarter of them asking the most (more than 8.6°) end closer 85 times in 100. The planner has the same flaw: it tunes 234 numbers per body, each on its own.

What are the options?

  • Radius handles, as in Unreal: one handle's pull spreads to its neighbours. Hand-designed: someone chooses each spread.
  • Goal babbling (Rolf, Steil and Gienger; standard in robotics): pick a random place for the hand to reach and let the controller reach for it, which gives coordinated reaches instead of random nudges.
  • A learned synergy network (this section's item): a small network learns, from moves the body really made, a short list of coordinated whole-body movements; practice moves and the planner pick from that list. Also a standard idea: "postural synergies" and "eigengrasps" in robot hands, "latent action spaces" in robot learning.

What we are doing, and why: the learned synergy network, because this plan chose learned synergies over hand-written schedules. Radius handles and goal babbling can be added; that is the operator's call.

What was built. A small network with two nonlinear hidden layers (this plan asks for 2 to 3) turns an intention of 32 numbers into a change of every body handle's target, relative to where the body is now. An intention of all zeros holds the pose. It learns in the loop, from hindsight: every order that ran its whole 250 ms is a sample of a change the body really made, and it counts in proportion to how well the order was followed and how much the body moved. The planner searches the 32 numbers instead of 234 per body (--motor-synergy on). Babble draws the 32 numbers at random, at today's average size of 45.7° of turn per handle (--synergy-babble on). With --smooth-source executed, the smoother blends each new order with the order the body really got, instead of with an average of proposals most of which were never sent.

Why 32. On 9,592 recorded orders, 32 directions carry 89 of every 100 parts of the posture changes. A network of that size reproduced 83% of later changes it had not seen (0% = no better than asking for no change, 100% = the whole change).

Does it help yet? Not in the one check so far. In the builder's physics check (1 body at rest, 20 trials per case), orders the trained synergy wrote were followed as well as separate random turns, and less well than a pose the body has held. That check used the environment's direction gauge; its count form is not yet measured. The reason found: the network learned to reproduce the changes the body made, and those changes, taken from other poses, act like separate turns from the rest pose. What it should learn from instead (for example only well-followed orders) is a decision for the lead.

Is this standard robotics? The parts are standard: IK handles, motor babbling, synergies, planning with a learned model of the body. What is different is the whole: no demonstrations, no reward, no trained policy; the model picks its own goals from its wellness and plans with its own predictions (spec section 1). Most robotics either hand-writes the controller for a known task or trains a policy with rewards.

Can it give exact, articulated movement from intentions? Yes, if three layers work together; the synergy network alone would not:

  1. The IK solver: for a reachable target the joint solution is exact; the limits are physical (strength, fatigue, gravity, things in the way).
  2. The planner must choose the right target, which needs the model to predict correctly what an order will do. Today it does not: the probes found its predictions did not change when its orders changed, and in the builder's planner check the planner closed 0.22% of the distance to its goal (0% = no closer, 100% = arrived). That is why the dead parts are repaired first.
  3. The synergy network gives coordinated whole-body movement from a short list of numbers; a short list cannot spell out every precise pose, so alone it gives smooth but approximate movement.

Precision stays possible because the planner may add a small correction to each individual part on top of the synergy (--synergy-residual-l2), at a cost, so it is used only where the coarse movement misses. It is built and off by default: the builder could not measure what it buys while the model's predictions ignore the order (knowing the real order changes their error by less than 0.08%, where 0% = the order makes no difference). That is this plan's own path: "Early control can be broad and coupled; finer independent control should emerge as prediction improves." It would be stopped by predictions that keep ignoring orders, a correction cost set too high, or the body's physical limits.

How we will know. Two measurements:

  1. The following count above, run on the live orders: whether orders written through the synergy end closer more often than today's 54 in 100, toward the held-pose count of 87.
  2. The body probe gives each limb an order several times and measures, in centimetres, how far it ends from where it was told; then it repeats the test after first turning the shoulder 10 degrees away (gof_probe_body.py, PRE_TURN_DEG). Exact control means that distance shrinks to the body's own natural wobble and stays there after the shoulder turn. That wobble, the finish line, has not been measured yet.

20. How Fast Should Primitive Control Appear?

Plan text (v2, unchanged)

For simple simulator mappings such as “this command moves this joint this way,” it is reasonable to expect an artificial model to learn much faster than a human infant because the simulator can provide dense, repeated, perfectly logged experience.

That does not mean all development should happen instantly. Coordinated multi-step behavior, body-wide balance, and cross-modal generalization are harder.

If basic action-conditioned prediction and repeatable control are still not improving after substantial simulated experience, do not simply wait longer. Treat that as evidence that the action signal, scaling, architecture, motor mapping, or wellness coupling may be wrong.

Liveness: parts that stopped responding, since H84's first day

measured repair planned after the loss-balance build

A sweep of every learned part on 24 lived moments per checkpoint, at seven checkpoints of the lineage. The table counts, out of every 100 units of a layer, how many responded on some moment (0 = the layer outputs a constant whatever it sees, 100 = every unit responds). A unit responds when its input leaves the flat low end of its activation function (GELU, the curve that turns a unit's input into its output). In that flat end, a change in the unit's input moves its output by less than 1.2% of the change, and the lesson's correction reaches the unit's weights shrunk by the same share; for a large input the output moves as much as the input (100%).
