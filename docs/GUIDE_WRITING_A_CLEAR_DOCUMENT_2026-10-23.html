Writing a page the operator can read

What this guide is. The method for writing a page about this project so that a reader who has not seen the project follows every sentence on one pass. It is built from two real pages about the same event, one left-arm movement of the simulated infant body MIMo that went the wrong way:

  • The rejected page: H84_BODY_REPAIR_EXPLAINED_2026-10-21 - Copy.html, the version of 9:23 AM PDT, October 23. The operator read it sentence by sentence and rejected it. Those comments are quoted in full in section 7 and beside each example in section 3.
  • The accepted page: H84_BODY_REPAIR_EXPLAINED_v2_2026-10-23.html, written new the same day to meet those comments, after two independent hostile reads. It is the better example, not a perfect one; section 6 lists what it still gets wrong.

Every sentence quoted on this page is copied from one of those two files or from the operator's messages. The rules in the style guide (HARD FORM, MECHANISM NEVER METAPHOR, ONE WORD PER THING, the writing rules, the reading gate) stand under everything here; this guide shows what they look like in a page.

1 • The reader and the test

Write for a reader who has not seen the project, was not there, and reads from the top once.

The operator put the test in one line: "Would a fifth grader that doesn't know about the October 21 screenshot understand this. Of course not." The reader to write for is that fifth grader: a careful person who has the page's own words and nothing else. Not the operator, who took the screenshot and remembers it. Not the other AI session, which read the same files. Not the writer.

The test, applied to every sentence: with only what the page has said above this sentence, can that reader say what each word refers to, what was measured, and why it is here? If any word fails, the sentence fails. The operator's own review in section 7 is this test run on the rejected page, and it failed on the title, on the first paragraph, on the status box and on most of the sentences after them.

Why this reader. A page that depends on what the reader remembers costs the reader a search of memory at every sentence. The operator named the cost: a forward reference "is requiring the reader to switch context which increases cognitive load". A page written for the fifth grader has no such cost for anyone, the operator included. The style guide's reading gate scores that cost from 1 to 10 and passes only 1 or 2.
2 • The sequence of a page

A page is built like a textbook chapter: the thing first, then its parts, then the story, then the state of the work.

The operator's instruction: "When writing this document one should have thought about how a text book for a course is constructed." A textbook never uses a word before the paragraph that defines it, never opens with the exam results, and never points the student to chapter 6 to understand chapter 1. The accepted page follows this sequence; the rejected page opened with a status box full of names the reader had not met.

Rule 16. The rest of the style guide, as it appears on a page.

  • One word per thing. The accepted page's second review found "torque and motor control; block and run; lesson and loss term" used for the same things and made each one word. A component is called by the component manifest's name; where the code's name differs, the code name is given once in code style and the canonical name used after.
  • Mechanism, never a person. No part "wants", "asks", "thinks" or "tells". The accepted page says "the planner search hands one of them a command and reads back the predicted effect", "the search could lower a predicted score by changing a number that changed nothing in the motor request".
  • Every number with its two ends. "0 means every part at the goal. Each of the two averages reads 1 when every axis is one typical movement off, so 2 means every position and every turn is one typical movement off."
  • Statuses with their facts. Not "checked" but "16 conditions at 6:45 PM PDT (that no changed command alters those numbers or sends a gradient through them...), then 27 conditions at 11:58 PM". A failed condition is given in the condition's own words, with what was measured instead, the cause and why it matters, never as a label standing in for it.
  • No time estimates. A promise such as "within 30 minutes" is removed; the trigger that ends a wait and the measured run time of the last run are stated instead.
  • Clock times as AM/PM Pacific, from the clock or a file's time, never from memory.
  • No magnitude adverbs ("slightly", "substantially", "a bit"): the number and its scale, or nothing.
  • Each fact traces to a file. The sources section names the file for every number, and the writer read that file while writing, not an earlier message.
4 • Pictures and diagrams

Render it, look at it as the hostile reader, and only then decide it explains something.

Operator: "Changing the dropdown doesn't give the reader a good sense of anything. You should have rendered the images and examined as an adversarial reader and determined that these illustrations impart almost no understanding. Maybe it need not only a better illustration but some type of animation."
  • Every mark is labelled, and the quantity is on the picture. The rejected page's section 2 offered a dropdown of seven joints and three whole-body pictures with one black axis line; the angles were in a separate table. The accepted page's section 6 draws, inside the MuJoCo scene before rendering, each joint's axis colored by its motor control (red at full, grey at none), rods for the direction the command asked each part to point, balls for the hand point, the goal's hand point and the command's requested position, and yellow points where the body touches the floor; its legend names each mark, and its table gives every joint angle at every 50 ms beside the pictures.
  • Show change over time as frames the reader can step through. The movement is 250 ms long; the accepted page gives its six saved records as a player (Play, or step), with the goal pose always on the right for comparison. A single before-and-after pair hides the path.
  • A diagram of the parts is laid out on a grid: boxes and arrow labels placed with gutters wide enough for the measured text, a rendered screenshot checked, then the grid removed. No label may overlap a box or run past the edge. The accepted page's diagram needed its columns widened and two arrows rerouted after the first screenshots showed a label on an arrow and a dashed line through a box.
  • Never invent an illustration. A made-up example (the first revision of the plan page showed "12 cm / 7 cm / 3 cm, example only") teaches the reader a number that was never measured. Draw the real case from its saved records, or draw nothing.
  • Look at the screenshots. A real browser at desktop width (1440 px) and phone width (390 px): every image loads, no sideways scroll, no overlap, no broken link, no script error. The check writes its receipt (the page's hash, the time, the viewport sizes, the broken anchors, the table row counts) beside the page. The tool in use: docs/h84_body_repair_v2_20261023/browser_check_page.py, which takes the page and its section ids and writes the screenshots and the receipt.
5 • The review before sending

The review the operator gives is the review that was owed before sending. Run it first, and run it as a stranger.

Operator: "Much better review needs to happen. It should be review like I've given."

The method, in the sequence it is run:

  1. Your own hostile read, sentence by sentence, written down. Read the whole draft as the fifth grader. For each sentence, write in a review note what fails: the undefined word, the forward reference, the missing why, the number without its meaning, the invented term, the superfluous line. The operator's section 7 below is the model for this note.
  2. An independent reader who did not write the page. Give it the draft, its screenshots, the operator's critique and the style guide, and the exact questions: terms used before definition; forward references; sentences a fifth grader with the page's definitions could not follow; assertions without a why; superfluous sentences; numbers that disagree with their source file; vague size words and statuses without facts; mechanisms described as persons; two names for one thing; decisions misstated; time estimates. Ask for a cognitive-load score from 1 to 10 and the three changes that would most lower it. Give it the code, not your conclusion.
  3. Every number against its file, now. For each number on the page, open the file that holds it and compare. The claim ledger (.builder/hooks/claim_check.py) does this mechanically for quoted facts. The second review of the accepted page found the goal's age counted from the wrong record and the score's scale off by a factor of 2; both had been written from memory.
  4. The browser check of section 4, on the version that will be sent, with its receipt recorded.
  5. Apply every finding, then re-review. The accepted page went through three drafts. A finding that is answered rather than changed is recorded with its reason.
  6. Record the review on the page: who read it, when, how many findings, what was applied, and the receipt files, so the next reader can see what the page has survived.

The hooks (.builder/hooks/worklog_time_check.py on every page written, .builder/hooks/reply_lint.py on chat) are the mechanical floor: they refuse the banned words and the person-verbs. They cannot tell whether a sentence has its why, or whether a term was defined above it. Only the read can.

8 • The checklist

Before a page is sent, every line reads yes.

  1. The first paragraph says what the system is, what we are trying to do, and which parts are involved.
  2. The people and sessions the page names are introduced in one sentence each.
  3. Every picture the page talks about is on the page, with the tool that made it, what it recorded, and a label on every panel and every mark.
  4. Every component is defined, by the project's name, with its input, computation and output, before the first sentence that uses it; a diagram shows how they connect.
  5. No sentence depends on a later section. Pointers to later detail appear only after the thing is already explained.
  6. No term was invented; where a real term is used (forward kinematics, loss term, token), it has a one-line definition at first use and keeps that one name.
  7. Every fact says what it shows about the problem; every assertion has its why or is deleted.
  8. Every number carries its meaning and the two ends of its scale in the same sentence; every result is a three-way comparison (start, predicted, measured) with its distances, never a direction word alone.
  9. Every status line gives the thing in plain words, what was measured, the value, the line it is judged against and the time; no time estimates anywhere.
  10. Every promised question is answered with the fact it asks for, or with "unmeasured" in those words.
  11. Every number was checked against its source file while writing; the sources section names the file for each.
  12. No software is described as a person; no banned synonym; no magnitude adverb; clock times in AM/PM Pacific from the clock.
  13. Your own sentence-by-sentence hostile read is written down and applied.
  14. An independent reader who did not write the page has read it with the questions of section 5 and scored it; every finding is applied or answered with a reason.
  15. The real-browser check at desktop and phone width passed on the version being sent, and its receipt is recorded.
  16. The review record on the page says who read it, when, what they found and what was done.
