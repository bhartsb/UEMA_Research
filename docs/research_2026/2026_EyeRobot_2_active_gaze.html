EyeRobot 2.0: choosing where to look as a motor action, and acting in the frame of what you are looking at

Kush Hari, Justin Kerr, Nidhya Shivakumar, Samarth Mahapatra, Carmelo Sferrazza, Jiahui Lei, Jitendra Malik, C. Karen Liu, Ken Goldberg and Angjoo Kanazawa (Amazon FAR; UC Berkeley; Stanford). Preprint; project page eyerobot2.github.io. arXiv 2610.03710, 2 October 2026.

The short version

The idea: most precise robot manipulation uses cameras bolted to the wrists. These authors replace them with one fixed stereo camera and two "virtual eyes" that swivel (by image warping) to fixate a 3D point the robot chooses. The images are processed foveally, with more attention near the fixation point. Where to look is learned as an action, and the arm's commands are expressed relative to where the eyes are looking.

What it showed: with passive stereo alone, success fell from 52% to 27%; with active fixation it came back to match wrist cameras when those were clear (69% vs 64%) and doubled them when a grasped object blocked the wrist view (48% vs 22%). Expressing actions in the gaze frame rather than the world frame was worth 21 points on its own.

Why it matters to us: UEMA already treats gaze as a limb (handle 23) with a 5-degree fovea and a panorama. This paper is the strongest 2026 evidence that the choice is right, and it adds one idea the spec does not have: command the hand in the coordinates of the fixation point.

What the paper did

The problem they set out to solve

Nearly all bimanual manipulation systems rely on wrist-mounted cameras for precision. Colocating a camera with the gripper is limiting: grasped objects occlude it, it constrains gripper design, it blurs with motion, and it creates a domain gap with human egocentric video. With only a passive fixed stereo camera, standard behaviour-cloning policies lose much of their performance. The paper asks whether human-like active fixation can replace wrist cameras, and how gaze should be chosen without hand-labelled gaze.

What they built

One calibrated stereo camera sits between the arms. Because rotating a camera about its optical centre only requires warping the image, "virtual eyes" are re-aimed at any 3D fixation point without 3D reconstruction, so gaze can be rendered counterfactually on top of existing teleoperation recordings. Fixation-centred images go through a pyramid of centred crops at several resolutions into a frozen vision transformer, so more tokens land near the centre. Gaze is hierarchical: a low-level gaze policy, trained by reinforcement learning on paused frames with a reward of negative distance between the fixation point and the named object's 3D centroid, outputs discrete deltas in azimuth, elevation and fixation depth (119 bins) at 30 Hz; a small target selector picks which object to fixate and when, rewarded by how much the fixation helps the arm policy's action prediction. The arm policy is behaviour cloning whose proprioception and action chunks are expressed in the fixation frame, with the fixation distance as a depth anchor.

  • Hardware: two 7-DoF arms, one stereo camera 35 cm above the base, 120-degree field of view per eye at 1600 by 1200.
  • Data: 1,278 demonstrations, about 288 minutes, over 7 real and 6 simulated tasks; over 1,000 real trials for evaluation.
  • Fixation accuracy about 3.5 cm; end-to-end throughput about 20 Hz for the active-fixation policies.

What they measured

  • Removing wrist cameras from standard policies: real success 52% to 27%. Active fixation beat passive stereo by 40 points in the real world and 20 in simulation across 13 tasks.
  • Versus wrist cameras: 69% vs 64% when wrist views were clear; 48% vs 22% when grasped objects occluded them.
  • Precision tasks: marker insertion 4% (passive) to 68% (active); straw insertion 8% to 44%, on the same camera stream.
  • Real ablations: removing foveation cost 20 points (66.5% to 46.5%); removing stereo depth cost 18.
  • Simulated ablations: expressing actions in the world frame instead of the fixation frame cost 21 points and put the method back on par with no gaze at all; fixating the wrong object throughout cost 24.
  • Gaze behaviour: every simulated policy learned to look first at the object the robot grasps first; gaze switched to a second object on handover tasks and stayed on the first on pot and spoon tasks.

Where it touches UEMA

Each box names a part of the UEMA specification and says, in plain terms, what the paper has to say about it.

§6.4 Gaze head and handle 23

UEMA emits gaze as azimuth and elevation from a learned query over forward-model latents. EyeRobot 2.0 does the same with a third output, fixation depth, and discretises the deltas. Their reason for discretising (breaking ties between duplicate objects) is a mild argument for a categorical gaze output alongside the continuous one.

§3.2 Foveated vision and §24.3 vision is 96% of compute

The paper's foveation (a pyramid of centred crops through a frozen encoder) was worth 20 points on real tasks. UEMA's fovea plus panorama is a stronger version of the same allocation. The paper's result is evidence that the compute-driven choice in §24.4 is also a capability-driven choice.

§12 Grounded anticipation, gaze dwell

The learned policies fixated the object about to be grasped before grasping it, which is precisely the gaze-dwell metric §12.1 lists as observable motor preparation. EyeRobot 2.0's selector reward (fixate where it helps action prediction) is a mechanism by which that anticipation could be learned rather than supervised.

§3.4 Physics handles, target_position in world coordinates

The paper's largest single lesson: commands expressed relative to the fixation point beat commands in a fixed world frame by 21 points. UEMA's handle targets are world positions. A fixation-relative command frame is a representational change the spec does not consider.

§11.1 Self as motor-correlation boundary

Gaze that tracks the hand or the object changes what correlates with motor commands in vision. If commands are expressed in the gaze frame, the correlation structure the body schema reads becomes gaze-dependent. This is a subtlety to keep in view if idea 4 is tried.

§1 Core thesis

Gaze here is learned by reinforcement learning with a hand-written reward, and the arm is a behaviour-cloned policy. Neither transfers. What transfers is the finding that gaze-as-action and gaze-relative commands matter, which UEMA can realise through the wellness gradient instead.

How it could help us

1. Test a fixation-relative command frame on the reach scene. Express each handle target as an offset from the point the eyes are fixating, convert to world coordinates for the physics, and compare reach success with the current world-frame targets from restored identical states. The paper's 21-point gap is the effect size to look for. This is a representational change in the motor encoder and the structured motor head, nothing else.

2. Add fixation depth to the gaze head. Azimuth and elevation say where; a third number says how far, and it anchored the paper's depth reasoning. MIMo's binocular eyes give vergence as the natural signal.

3. Record gaze dwell as an ability recheck. Time the fovea spends on the ball before the hand arrives is the paper's clearest emergent behaviour and the spec's own anticipation metric. It can be measured on the saved model today.

4. Use counterfactual gaze rendering for Stream 3. The paper renders any gaze on top of recorded demonstrations because the camera is fixed. In the simulator UEMA can do the same: replay a demonstration with different gaze sequences and let the forward model learn what looking does.

What to be careful about

Everything is task-specific imitation learning plus reinforcement learning with a geometric reward, on a real robot with a single fixed camera; none of the components fit UEMA's no-reward, no-policy design. Head and neck are not controlled; only the eyes. Gaze is object-level only. Simulation did not reproduce the foveation or stereo gains, which the authors attribute to missing visual complexity, so UEMA's MuJoCo scenes may also understate them. Throughput halved (40 Hz to 20 Hz) with active fixation. Code is promised but not yet released.

In the authors' words

Fixating on a dynamic task-relevant 3D point acts as a form of physical attention which centers input observations, compacts the data distribution, filters irrelevant visual regions, and allocates additional resolution where it matters for fine-grained tasks without the cost of processing the full panorama at high resolution.
