Reach the target, then stay there: the plan

The setting. MIMo is a simulated infant body that a physics program, MuJoCo, moves when its motors push. UEMA is the learning program that controls MIMo. Every 50 ms (one model step) UEMA reads MIMo's senses and predicts what will happen next. Every 250 ms (5 model steps) it sends the motors a command and holds it for those 5 model steps. A command is a table of 26 rows × 9 numbers: 25 rows for the body parts and one for the eyes (the code calls a row a handle). Each row holds where that part should be (x, y, z in cm), which way it should turn (4 numbers naming one 3-D turn), a radius that the body head reads and no motor uses, and a strength from 0 to 1 that sets how hard the motors push for that part. The project's goal is useful movement: reaching an object, touching it, grasping it, rolling over.

The parts of UEMA this page names. The motor controller is a network that outputs a command from the body's present state; its output is called the first command. The body head is a network that reads the command and the transformer's output (the transformer is UEMA's core network) and outputs each body part's position change (cm) and turn (degrees) after the command's 250 ms. The planner starts from the first command, changes it 20 times, scores each version by what the body head predicts for it, and sends the lowest-scoring version if its predicted score is below both the start pose's score and the first command's predicted score; otherwise it sends the first command unchanged (the record's label for that outcome: fallback). The observer recorder (the code's observer) saves, every model step, each body part's position and turn, the joint angles, the motor controls, the contacts, the command and the planner's own numbers; one such saved set is a record.

The task this plan is about. The body is at pose S. An earlier saved record T (the target) is chosen by the planner from its memory of the last 200 model steps. Move both hands to where they are in T, and keep them there. Better predictions matter only if they produce that movement.

The measure, used on every line of this page. D = the straight-line distance, in cm, from a hand's hand point (a fixed point inside the palm, the hand body's origin; the recorder's name for it is the palm point) to the same point in T. One D per hand per command, read three ways: at the start of the command, as the body head predicted it would be at the end, and as measured at the end. Arrived = both hands' D at or below 2.0 cm by model step 40 of a measurement (2 s). Stayed = after arriving, both hands at or below 2.0 cm at every one of the next 20 model steps (1 s). The 2.0 cm and the 20 model steps were proposed by the builder at 6:05 PM and accepted by the overseer at 6:06 PM as fixed working choices for this diagnostic; they are not measured limits of the body. The exact distances are always shown beside the counts, so a near miss cannot hide behind a label. Beside the two-hand counts the page reports one-hand results, the whole-body error E (one number over all 25 body parts, 0 = every part exactly where it is in T; its formula is fixed in each measurement's package before the run, and the package states whether it is the planner's pose-error score defined in section 2 or a plain mean distance), falls, and contacts with anything other than the floor, because hands at the target do not by themselves show that the whole pose is right.

Who does what. Two AI sessions work beside the operator. The builder writes the code and runs the checks and training. The overseer reviews the evidence and, under the operator's delegation of October 13, decides what may run; its decisions are quoted on this page from its decision files. The operator decides what the operator's own rules reserve.

3 • Why reaching has not happened

Four measured facts, and one missing measurement.

  1. Half the commands are not chosen for the target. Every second 250 ms command is the motor controller's command with babble, and it displaces the hand a median 1.69 cm with no net direction (section 2).
  2. A planner command can describe a body that cannot exist. Each of its 25 body rows is a pose for one part on its own, so rows can contradict each other. In the command behind the viewer image the operator sent on October 21 (run POSE_A_off_494025, body 3, model step 494,810), the left forearm's requested turn needed an elbow angle 57.8° beyond the elbow's range, and the two knees' requested turns 76.4° and 75.5° beyond theirs. The program that turns rows into motor controls (the command execution path) solves for the arm's seven joint angles, inside their ranges, that make the wrist row's position error and the three arm rows' turn errors smallest (the upper arm's and forearm's rows carry no position the motors use); when the rows contradict, no joint setting satisfies any row exactly, so the motors are driven toward a pose that matches none of the requests.
  3. The body head's predictions are wrong by a measured amount. Over the 66 commands the planner changed, the predicted pose-error score was below the measured one by a median 0.634 (over all 72, 0.57); the median start score was 7.27, so the typical miss is 9 of 100 of a typical start's score. The measured left hand ended farther from T than its predicted D in 38 of 72 commands, the right in 41. The planner sends whatever this body head scores lowest.
  4. A command that describes T exactly may still move the body slowly, and how slowly is known from one start only. On October 21 (run seg41j, body 5, Tests row whole_body_request_limb_control_483120), each arm and leg motor was given its target joint angle directly, not through the 26-row command. After 250 ms the right arm's mean joint error had fallen from 14.4° to 3.8° (73 of 100 of the gap closed) while the left arm's had risen from 23.7° to 26.8° (the gap grew by 13 of 100); over that 250 ms the chest's holding load read 1.81, where 1.0 = exactly the torque the chest's motors can give and 1.81 = holding the pose needs 1.81 times that, so the chest could not hold it. A September 25 test on other starts read 49 to 66 of 100 of a reachable angle gap closed in 250 ms. The readings disagree, and one start is not a measurement of the body.
  5. The planner has never been compared with the simplest command. The simplest command is the direct command: T's own positions and turns, row by row, sent to the motors and held. Its measured D is a baseline, not the best movement the body could ever make (a sequence of different poses could do better); it separates difficulty moving the body from difficulty choosing commands. Without it, "44 of 72 nearer" cannot be judged against anything.

5 • How a candidate repair is assessed without hiding setbacks

Same starts, same targets, same length; both hands; margins set before the comparison.

  • Use the same starts, targets and observation length for the candidate and its comparison. Show each hand's final D and its worst setback during the measurement, the arrived-and-stayed counts, the whole-body error E and the contact problems.
  • A correction is not kept because half the examples improve or tie: the size of improvements and of setbacks matters. The acceptable harm and the required improvement are set before the comparison, from the measured spread between repeats, and written in the Tests row.
  • An average prediction error below any line is not proof that the body head ranks commands well. Rank is tested directly: predicted against measured D for alternative commands from the same start.
  • A short comparison shows a candidate's immediate effect. Repeat on new starts and on later saved learning states to measure repeatability and retention before any broad ability claim.
  • Targets are not filtered out by a static holding load alone: a different route or a different support can make a pose reachable. A failed arm fit (the inverse-kinematics fit of October 23, whose calibration refused itself) does not by itself show that the model's architecture must be replaced.
